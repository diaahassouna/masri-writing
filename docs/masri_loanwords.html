Masri Tier 2 Loanword Policy

A rigorous, internally consistent standard for spelling loanwords in Masri Tier 2 — when to keep a foreign pronunciation, when to Egyptianize it, how to derive one canonical spelling instead of unlimited free variation, and how integration into Egyptian grammar works. Cross-checked against assets/data/tier2-rules.json and the letter inventory on the Alphabet page; builds on the verb and noun morphology in the Grammar Reference.

Masri Tier 2 Loanword Policy Extends Masri Tier 2 — a phonemic, near-phonemic Latin/Coptic/Greek-based orthography for Egyptian Arabic — to loanwords, without turning it into a transliteration scheme for the donor language and without opening the door to unlimited spelling variation.

Status: first pass, drafted for review, same convention as the Grammar Reference. Populates loanword_examples and adds formal spelling_rules ids 12–16 to assets/data/tier2-rules.json. Deliberately does not add anything to extra_letters in either data file — see §4 and §13, rule M4: no new Tier 2 letters are introduced for this.

0. Design goals

  1. Encode pronunciation, not source spelling. A Tier 2 loanword spelling represents how the reference Cairene speaker actually says the word, never how it's spelled in English, French, or any other donor language.
  2. Support both retained-foreign and Egyptianized pronunciations — both are real, attested things Cairene speakers do — decided by lexical status and attested pronunciation, never by how "English" a word looks in writing.
  3. One canonical spelling per loanword sense, with genuinely attested secondary pronunciations recorded as explicit accepted variants — never silent, uncontrolled variation.
  4. Reuse the extra letters Tier 2 already has (P, V, C, and J — see the Alphabet page) rather than inventing new ones, per the site's existing "no new letters" design principle. P, V, and C are loanword-specific; J is shared with two native uses (Upper Egyptian Ṣaʿidi ج and MSA ج), so a loanword /ʒ/ simply reuses a letter that already exists for other reasons.
  5. A graded nativization threshold, not a binary foreign/native switch — loanwords visibly sit at different points along the same continuum, and the policy should say so explicitly instead of forcing every word into one of two boxes.

1. Core principle

Masri Tier 2 primarily encodes pronunciation — specifically the reference Cairene speaker's actual, attested pronunciation of the word, in whatever register it's being used — filtered through Egyptian phonological structure only where that structure is actually applied in speech, never imposed on a word that isn't pronounced that way. This is the same standard the rest of Masri already holds native vocabulary to (spelling documents what's said, not an idealized "correct" form); loanwords don't get a different rule, they get the same rule applied to a wider, more variable set of attested pronunciations.

When principles conflict, this ranking applies (expanded into a full hierarchy at §12):

  1. Attested pronunciation
  2. Phonemic contrast Tier 2 already provides a letter for (don't collapse p/v/j into b/f/g just because the word is foreign)
  3. Lexical/morphological transparency
  4. Source-language spelling resemblance — lowest priority, and not a goal in its own right.

One clarification worth stating up front: the site's existing design principle "orthography over phonetics where the two conflict" (e.g. the definite article always spelled el regardless of sun/moon-letter assimilation) governs a specific, closed set of native morphological spellings. It is not license to spell loanwords by their donor-language letters — for loanwords, pronunciation stays on top.

3. Retained vs. Egyptianized pronunciation

The choice between a retained-foreign and an Egyptianized pronunciation is governed by attested pronunciation and lexical status (§2) — never by whether the word looks English on the page.

Retained/foreign-leaning is the expected default for: Category 3–5 items in formal, technical, or naming contexts, and Category 6 items too new to have developed a settled Egyptianized form yet.

Egyptianized is the expected default for: Category 1 items, and any item once its dominant attested pronunciation in ordinary informal Cairene speech shows adapted phonology (consonant substitution, cluster simplification, vowel shift).

The mechanism is the same one Tier 2 already uses for native sound change: spelling documents attested pronunciation, and if the dominant pronunciation of a Category 3 term later shifts toward the Egyptianized form (attested, not assumed), its canonical spelling updates to match. The system never prescribes a target pronunciation for a loanword to "become more Egyptian" — it records what's actually said.

4. Adaptation rules

Masri Tier 2 does not assume every foreign sound must be converted to its closest Egyptian neighbor. Where Tier 2 already has a letter for the foreign phoneme, that contrast is preserved by default (see §1's ranking); only where no letter exists does the sound fold into something else, and even then a digraph or existing allophonic pattern is preferred over inventing a new letter.

5. Decision algorithm

A precise enough procedure that two people applying it independently should usually reach the same result:

  1. How does the reference Cairene speaker actually pronounce the word — attested, not assumed? Same evidentiary standard the rest of Masri already holds native words to.
  2. Does that pronunciation already differ from the source-language pronunciation (consonant substitution, cluster simplification, vowel or stress shift)? If there's no adaptation at all, treat the word as retained/foreign-leaning for now; if it's adapted, continue.
  3. What's the word's lexical status (§2's seven categories, diagnosed via §11)? This sets the default register expectation the attested form is checked against — the attested form from step 1 always wins if it conflicts with the category default, since pronunciation sits at the top of the conflict hierarchy (§12).
  4. Has the word taken native Egyptian derivational morphology (feminine, plural, diminutive, verbal derivation)? If so, mark it Category 1 or 7 regardless of its phonological adaptation, and permit (not require) phonological nativization to accompany it.
  5. Assemble the canonical Tier 2 spelling from steps 1–4 using the phoneme-mapping table at §4. If a second pronunciation is also genuinely common, record it as an explicit accepted variant (§6) — never as a silent alternative.

6. Canonical spelling architecture

Recommended model: one canonical, attested-default spelling per loanword sense, plus explicitly flagged accepted variant(s) where a second pronunciation is genuinely common — never silent, unlimited variation. This generalizes a convention already present in loanword_examples (e.g. "pizza / pitza" as accepted forms, "bizza" explicitly marked wrong) into a formal rule (spelling_rules id 16, §13).

Three alternative architectures considered and rejected:

  • One canonical spelling only, no variants: too rigid — falsely marks real attested variation (pizza/pitza) as an error, or buries a genuine register distinction (kombyuter/computer).
  • Fully free variation: exactly the "uncontrolled spelling variation" problem this policy exists to prevent.
  • Dual-canonical by default (foreign-preserving and Egyptianized both always canonical): overcorrects — most loanwords don't actually have two live pronunciations, and forcing a pair on every entry manufactures variation that isn't attested. Reserved for the genuine, common cases where a formal/colloquial split really exists (typically Category 3).

7. Morphological integration

Derivational Egyptian morphology (gender, plural, diminutive, verbal derivation) applies to a loanword once it has reached Category 1 or 7 status — that is, once it functions as an ordinary noun or verb slot in an Egyptian sentence, not merely as a cited foreign form. There's no separate "loanword morphology": the same rules already in the Grammar Reference (§1 nouns, §2 plurals, §4 verbs) apply directly. The one loanword-specific point: a loanword noun without natural gender takes its gender by the same final-vowel-shape and semantic-analogy defaults native nouns already use — no special-casing needed once it's a lexical item.

Key question: once a loanword is fully integrated, should the stem itself get a canonical Egyptianized Tier 2 form? Yes — but as a form that coexists with, rather than replaces, the citation-form spelling. The base noun (e.g. pizza) keeps its own canonical entry, whatever attested pronunciation that is (possibly retained-p per §4); its derived forms (bitzaya, bitzat) are separately canonicalized once they have an attested dominant pronunciation. These can genuinely diverge — p in the citation form, b in the derived form — because in practice they function as different lexical entries (a cited foreign dish name vs. a fully nativized derived noun), and Masri's job is to spell each as it's actually said rather than force visual agreement between them.

8. Proper names and brands

Recommended model: structured dual representation, not a free-for-all.

  • The canonical Tier 2 spelling is the name's actual local Egyptian pronunciation, attested — same top-of-hierarchy rule as everything else in this policy (e.g. Pepsi → bebsi, §14).
  • A source-language reference form may be given alongside on first mention (in parentheses or a dedicated data field) purely for recoverability. This is documentation practice, not a competing orthographic form.
  • Exception: names whose entire social function is international recognizability — a company's own registered Latin-script logotype, a person's own preferred spelling of their name — may be cited in their original form when directly quoting or labeling that specific entity, the same way English prose still writes "iPhone" with a capital P mid-sentence. That's a quotation, not a Masri spelling.
  • Personal names of Egyptians themselves are native vocabulary, not loanwords, and are simply spelled per the Grammar Reference like any other word.

10. Other source languages

The underlying policy (§1–§6) is language-agnostic; donor-language identity by itself never determines category or spelling — only attested pronunciation, frequency, and morphological behavior do. The notes below are empirical tendencies per donor, not separate rules:

  • French: historically the older, more deeply integrated European donor (otobees, garaaj, asanseer, ϣofeer). Many French loans are old enough to sit solidly at Category 1, and /ʒ/ in this layer nativizes to hard g (§4's "older loans" branch) rather than ϣ. French nasal vowels collapse to the nearest oral Masri vowel.
  • Italian: mostly Category 1–2 food, craft, and household vocabulary (pizza, vitrina); generally short, low-cluster words that Egyptianize easily.
  • Greek: mostly very old, deeply nativized loans (terabeeza "table," from trapezi) that predate the modern code-switching layer entirely — treated as Category 1 by default, spelled purely by long-established Egyptian pronunciation with no synchronic Greek phonology preserved.
  • Turkish: a substantial Ottoman-period layer, almost entirely Category 1 already (afandi, kubri, tarboosh) — spelled per Egyptian pronunciation with no live link back to Turkish vowel harmony or phonology.
  • Persian: a smaller, older, often indirect layer (frequently via Ottoman Turkish or Classical Arabic) — treated the same as the oldest Greek/Turkish loans, sometimes indistinguishable in behavior from native vocabulary (dulab, §14).
  • Spanish: minimal direct borrowing into Egyptian Arabic. Where a plausible candidate exists it's typically media-mediated and marginal (Category 6) rather than a settled loan — see §15's stress-test entry 30 for how the policy handles this "empty category" case honestly rather than manufacturing an example.

11. Nativization threshold

Nativization is modeled as graded, not binary — a very new tech term and a centuries-old Turkish loan aren't in the same state, and treating them identically would either over-nativize the new one or under-nativize the old one. Each axis below is scored Low / Medium / High from attested usage; the categories in §2 are qualitative bands along this same continuum, not a separate decision.

AxisLow integrationMediumHigh integration
FrequencyRare, occasional code-switchCommon in a specific registerCommon in everyday general speech
Age of borrowingRecent (years)Established (a generation)Old (multiple generations/centuries)
Phonological adaptationRetains source phonemes closelyPartial adaptation (some substitution)Fully adapted to Egyptian phonology
Grammatical integrationCited as a fixed foreign formTakes at most one Egyptian process (e.g. a bare plural)Takes full Egyptian derivational morphology
Speaker perceptionPerceived as clearly foreignPerceived as borrowed but normalPerceived as ordinary Egyptian vocabulary; origin often unknown to most speakers
Pronunciation variabilityWide, unsettled variationNarrowing — a dominant + minority formSingle stable pronunciation
Lexical/dictionary statusNot lexicalized, ad hocLexicalized in slang/technical glossariesLexicalized as ordinary vocabulary

Banding: mostly-Low axes → Category 6 (recent borrowing); a mixed profile → Category 2/3 (code-switched/technical); mostly-High → Category 1 (fully integrated). This is a banding, not a formula — no single axis is decisive on its own (age alone doesn't guarantee integration: some old borrowings stayed marginal, some fast tech terms nativize within a couple of years), so the threshold is crossed when enough axes independently tip High that the word behaves, spells, and inflects like ordinary Egyptian vocabulary.

12. Conflict-resolution hierarchy

A first-pass hierarchy — actual pronunciation, phonemic contrast, lexical identity, morphological integration, source-language recoverability, visual recognizability — is close, but two changes make it more usable in practice:

  • Merge "lexical identity" and "morphological integration" into one combined tier: in practice they resolve together, since a word's derived-form behavior is how its lexical identity in Egyptian shows up (§7).
  • Explicitly demote "source-language recoverability" and "visual recognizability" from merely lower-priority to actively subordinate, so a writer doesn't unconsciously reach for source spelling as an early tiebreaker.

Revised, five-tier hierarchy:

  1. Attested actual Cairene pronunciation — never overridden by anything below.
  2. Phonemic contrast Tier 2 already encodes — use p/v/j/etc. where the sound is genuinely there; don't erase a real contrast for tidiness.
  3. Lexical identity and morphological integration together — does the word's derived-form behavior indicate further nativization (§7)?
  4. Source-language recoverability — a tiebreaker only, between two otherwise equally attested variants; never a deciding factor on its own.
  5. Visual resemblance to source spelling — lowest, a courtesy only, essentially never decisive given the constraints above.

13. Normative standard: Masri Tier 2 Loanword Standard

Mandatory

  • M1. Spelling encodes actual attested Cairene pronunciation, never source-language spelling.
  • M2. Every loanword gets exactly one canonical Tier 2 spelling per lexical status/register; a second attested pronunciation is recorded as an explicit accepted variant, never silently (§6).
  • M3. P and V are used whenever the corresponding foreign phoneme (/p/, /v/) is actually retained in the citation form's pronunciation; they are never silently replaced by B or F "to look more Egyptian." J is used whenever a loanword's /ʒ/ is retained — the same letter Ṣaʿidi and MSA registers already use for native ج — and is never silently replaced by G, which is reserved for the Cairene hard-/g/ reading.
  • M4. No new Tier 2 letters are introduced for loanword sounds Masri doesn't already have a letter for (/tʃ/, /ŋ/) — use the digraph/allophone rules at §4 instead. /ʒ/ is the one exception worth stating explicitly: Masri already has a letter for it, J (shared with native Ṣaʿidi/MSA ج), so a retained-foreign /ʒ/ may use J directly rather than only falling back to ϣ or g.
  • M5. Loanword θ→t / ð→d is kept distinct from the native θ→s / ð→z Cairene merger; the two are not the same rule and must not be conflated.
  • M6. Once a loanword takes native Egyptian derivational morphology, that derived form follows ordinary Masri grammar rules (Grammar Reference §1–§4) — there is no separate loanword morphology.

Default (apply unless a specific attested exception is documented)

  • D1. Word-initial and word-medial consonant clusters are retained as pronounced, not broken up by an inserted vowel.
  • D2. Proper names and brands are spelled per their actual local Egyptian pronunciation; a source-form reference may be added alongside for recoverability but isn't itself the canonical spelling.
  • D3. Technical/international terms default to their dominant ordinary-speech pronunciation as canonical, with a source-like form as an accepted formal-register variant where genuinely in use.
  • D4. Stress is not marked, for loanwords or native words alike.

Optional

  • O1. A source-language reference form may be included in glossary/dictionary entries alongside the canonical spelling, for recoverability — documentation, not an alternate orthography.
  • O2. Writers may deliberately use a more foreign-preserving pronunciation for stylistic or register effect, provided it's a pronunciation someone would actually say, not an invented one.

Prohibited

  • X1. Do not invent a spelling for a pronunciation nobody actually uses.
  • X2. Do not silently multiply spellings for the same attested pronunciation — use the canonical + variant mechanism (§6), not free-for-all.
  • X3. Do not use donor-language spelling as the basis for a Tier 2 spelling when it conflicts with actual Egyptian pronunciation.
  • X4. Do not add new Tier 2 letters to chase foreign phonemic precision Masri has decided not to represent.
  • X5. Do not force phonological nativization (p→b, v→f) on a citation-form loanword that hasn't reached full morphological integration (§7) — premature nativization erases the retained/Egyptianized distinction this policy exists to preserve.

16. Weaknesses and proposed improvements

The design brief explicitly asked not to just agree with its own assumptions. Six real issues, with proposed fixes:

  • Apparent contradiction between M3 and §7's nativization allowance. M3 says P/V/J are never silently replaced; §7 then allows p→b at full morphological integration. Read carelessly, these look inconsistent. Fix: M3 is explicitly scoped to the citation form (now stated directly in §13) — the derived form is a distinct lexical entry, not a "replacement" of the citation spelling.
  • "Attested pronunciation" is doing enormous load-bearing work throughout this policy, but no corpus or attestation source is defined. In practice, deciding what counts as "dominant" will often be a compiler's own judgment call — the same subjectivity problem the policy was built to eliminate, just relocated from spelling to pronunciation-selection. Fix: either document a specific reference source (a corpus, a defined speaker community) or explicitly acknowledge the judgment call and resolve disagreements the same way the Grammar Reference already does — with a documented, revisable decision, flagged the way that document flags genuinely open items.
  • Genuine 50/50 pronunciation ties aren't addressed. The canonical+variant model (§6) assumes a dominant form exists; a true tie (common right at the moment a tech term is adopted) isn't covered. Fix: allow a rare, explicitly-labeled "dual-canonical, unweighted" tag for this specific case, distinct from the ordinary canonical/variant pair, expiring once usage settles.
  • The 7-category status model (§2) and the graded threshold (§11) substantially overlap. Categories 1/2/3/6/7 are largely just verbal labels for different points on the same continuum §11 already grades, while 4/5 (names/brands) are a genuinely separate axis about naming, not integration. Fix: keep §2's seven-way taxonomy as descriptive/documentation vocabulary only; for actual spelling decisions, collapse 1/2/3/6/7 into three practical buckets — "ordinary vocabulary" (post-threshold), "unsettled/marginal" (pre-threshold), and "name" (§8) — driven directly by §11, not by a separate categorical judgment.
  • The /ʒ/ "older loans → g, newer → ϣ" rule (§4) has no crisp age boundary, and now sits alongside a third option (J, per M4's exception) with no clear line telling a compiler which of the three to reach for. Fix: drop the vague "old/new" language for the ϣ/g choice; default to ϣ unless the specific loan is already independently established as Category 1 with an attested hard-g pronunciation — i.e. let §11's threshold decide, not raw age — and reserve J for loanwords where the speaker is consciously equating the sound with native ج (Ṣaʿidi/MSA register) rather than simplifying it.
  • Source-recoverability genuinely conflicts with phonemic consistency for proper nouns and brands — a heavily Egyptianized brand spelling can obscure the name's international recognizability. The policy already resolves this correctly (pronunciation wins, recoverability is a documentation add-on, §8/§12), but that's a real, acknowledged cost, not a free win, and should be stated as such rather than glossed over.
