Three structural rules carry through every section below:

  1. El is a free particle. It is written separate from the word it modifies, in every tier, regardless of how it actually assimilates in speech (el shams, not esh-shams) — except when li- or bi- precedes it (rule 3 below).
  2. ma is a free particle too, but -sh(i) is bound to the verb. ma is written separately, like el; -sh(i) (and any object clitics between the verb and -sh(i)) fuses to the verb as one word — see §5.
  3. A closed set of short prepositions/particles fuse with el. li-, bi-, fi-, shortened a-/Ⲵa-, and topicalizing ma become lel/lil, bel, fel,al/Ⲵal, Mal as one word (but `and never fuses, and negation ma never fuses with the verb — see §5) — see §6.3.

1. Nouns — no case, no nunation

Egyptian Arabic nouns don't inflect for case, unlike Modern Standard Arabic. This is inherited wholesale by Masri: there is nothing to represent.

The one common survival is the frozen accusative in a handful of fixed expressions:

  • شكراً — "thank you" Tier 1: shokran Tier 2: ϣokran

Rule: Masri never marks i3rab (case endings). A word's Masri spelling reflects only what is actually pronounced in the colloquial, pausal form — matching how Egyptian Arabic itself treats nouns.

2. Plurals

2.1 Broken plurals

Broken plurals are template-based (the consonant skeleton stays, the vowel pattern changes). Because Masri's letter substitutions are consonant-level, broken-plural templates transfer automatically — you just apply the consonant table to whichever slots the template happens to land on.

None of the plural templates themselves need new rules; they're pure vowel-pattern spelling, already covered by the existing vowel table. The only judgment calls are per-word, same as with any singular: does this particular root contain ع/ح/ث/ذ/ش/ق?

2.1a Sound feminine plural (-aat)

Distinct from the broken-plural templates above: a closed class of feminine nouns pluralize by suffixing -aat (pronounced "aat," a long vowel) directly onto the singular stem, rather than reshuffling the internal vowel pattern.

Meaning Singular Plural Tier 1 / Tier 2
wife meraat wives meraat → merataat

This is the noun-side counterpart to the verb vowel-shortening rule in §5.1/spelling_rules id 7: attaching the -aat suffix shortens the stem's own long vowel (meraat → marat-) before the long -aat suffix is added — the same shortening that -ha, -na, -kom, and -hom trigger on this and other nouns (§3.2).

The rest of the color set (bunni "brown," ramaadi "gray," banafsigi "purple," etc.) are invariable nisba adjectives and need no special handling beyond normal Tier 1/Tier 2 letter substitution.

2.3 Dual number, and dual/plural of professional titles & nisba nouns

Egyptian Arabic keeps a genuine dual: a masculine singular takes -een, and the corresponding feminine singular (formed the regular way, by adding -a) takes -teen in place of that final -a. It shows up clearly on the professional/honorific titles already introduced in §7 and used as the dual-agreement example in §6.3 — worth spelling out the full paradigm here rather than leaving it to a single example.

Spelling note: the dual suffix is written with é — Ostazéén, not Ostazeen — the same "café / letter-name-A" vowel from §3.2's pronunciation note. This keeps it visually distinct from the ordinary sound masculine plural suffix, which is spelled with a plain ee (the "redeem" vowel): the two suffixes look identical in Arabic script (ـين either way) but are grammatically unrelated, so Masri spells them differently on purpose.

Shortening note: a stem's long vowel shortens before the dual suffix exactly as it already does before -aat and the "heavy" possessive clitics (spelling_rules id 7/9, §2.1a/§3.2) — Ostaaz shortens to Ostaz- before -één/-téén, the same pattern as béét→bétna.

3.2 Possessive clitics on nouns

Attaching to a noun that ends in a vowel vs. one or two consonants changes the suffix shape — this is pure phonology and doesn't interact with any Masri-specific rule beyond "spell what's actually said." One phonological effect does need an explicit rule, though: a medial/final long vowel in the noun shortens before the "heavier" clitic set — -ha (her), -na (our), -kom (your pl.), -hom (their) — while the "lighter" set — -i (my), -ak/-ik (your m./f.), -o (his) — leaves it intact. This is the same shortening already formalized for verbs in spelling_rules id 7 (§5.1), now generalized to nouns as spelling_rules id 9.

Pronunciation note: long e (spelled ee per this doc's doubling convention) is pronounced like the long e in the English word "redeem", while the letter é is pronounced like the English letter name "A," or like the é in café — worth keeping in mind when reading béét, bétha, etc. out loud.

بيت (béét) "house"

Tier 1 Tier 2
my house bééti bééti
your house (m) béétak béétak
your house (f) béétik béétik
his house bééto bééto
her house bétha bétha
our house bétna bétna
your house (pl) bétkom bétkom
their house béthom béthom

So the trigger class is: -ha, -na, -kom, -hom, and the sound plural -aat all shorten the stem; -i, -ak, -ik, -o do not. The heavier/lighter split lines up with person: 3rd fem. singular, 1st/2nd/3rd plural, and the plural marker all shorten; 1st singular, 2nd singular (both genders), and 3rd masc. singular don't. A second noun confirms the same split — زيت zeet "oil" and دقيق di'ee' (also spelled d'ee', with syncope of the initial i) "flour," combined with -na:

زيتنا في دقيقنا — "our oil is in our flour" Tier 1: Zetna fi d'e'na Tier 2: Zetna fi dⱯeⱯna

Both zeet→zetna and di'ee'/d'ee'→d'e'na shorten the long ee to a single e before -na, exactly like béét→bétna — and d'e'na additionally shows the same first-syllable syncope already documented for verbs (§5.1: kiber→keber), now confirmed on a noun too.

مراة (meraat-) "wife" (construct form) — chosen because the root's أ needs the mid-word Ɐ rule:

Tier 1 Tier 2
my wife meraati meraati
his wife meraato meraato
her wife meratha meratha

أب (abu-) "father" — irregular construct base, worth flagging because it keeps the old -u construct ending that MSA lost in colloquial elsewhere:

Tier 1 Tier 2

your father (m) abook abook

3.4 Suffixed prepositions

A representative set (fi "in," `ala "on," `and "to have"):

fi "in" `ala "on" `and "to have"
Arabic base في على عند
...me feyya / feyya `alayya / Ⲵalayya `andi / Ⲵandi
...you (m) feek / feek `aleek / Ⲵaleek `andak / Ⲵandak
...him fee(h) / fee(h) `alee(h) / Ⲵalee(h) `ando / Ⲵando
...her feeha / feeha `aleeha / Ⲵaleeha `andaha / Ⲵandaha

Shortened form: unsuffixed ala/Ⲵala ("on, about") can shorten toa/Ⲵa usually before el and elli — اللي, "which/that/who," the Egyptian colloquial equivalent of MSA الذي:

  • على اللي فات "about what happened/the past" → **a elli faat** / **Ⲵa elli faat** (rather than the fullerala elli faat / Ⲵala elli faat)

When the shortened a/Ⲵa precedes **el** specifically, the two go on to fuse into a single word — **a + el → `al / Ⲵa + el → Ⲵal** — the same pattern as li-+el→lel and bi-+el→bel (spelling_rules id 8, §6.3):

  • يللا ع القهوة (= يللا على القهوة) "come on, let's go for coffee" Tier 1: Yalla al ahwa.** (= fuller, unfused **Yallaala el ahwa.) Tier 2: Yalla Ⲵal ahwa. (= fuller, unfused Yalla Ⲵala el ahwa.)

ka "like, as": a comparison particle, spelled and pronounced the same in Tier 1 and Tier 2. Unlike fi-, `a-/Ⲵa-, and bi-, ka does not fuse with a following el — a surface kal would collide with the unrelated past-tense verb kal "ate," so the two words are always kept visually and phonetically distinct. Whether ka + el comes out as ka + el, ka + geminated-consonant, etc. depends on the ordinary sun/moon-letter assimilation the following noun's initial consonant triggers on the definite article "el" (§1) — ka itself never changes shape or absorbs the el:

في مسلسل النص التاني هتلاقي النص دايماً بيقول ان السقسوقة كالتالي.
fi mosalsal El Noṣṣ El Taani ha tlaaⱯi daymann in El Noṣṣ beyⱯool inn el saⱯsooⱯa ka el taali.

Wrong: fi mosalsal El Noṣṣ El Taani ha tlaaⱯi daymann in El Noṣṣ beyⱯool inn el saⱯsooⱯa kal taali. — fusing (ka + el) into kal here is incorrect, since it's indistinguishable from the past-tense verb kal "ate."

4. Verbs

4.1 Root-and-pattern basics

Egyptian Arabic verbs are built from a 3- or 4-consonant root plus a vowel/affix pattern. Since Masri's substitution table operates at the consonant level, this system transfers cleanly: whichever slot in the pattern the root's ع/ح/ث/ذ/ش/ق lands in, apply the same substitution you'd apply anywhere else.

The dictionary-citation form is the 3rd person masculine singular past (Arabic has no infinitive) — e.g. كتب katab "he wrote," cited as katab/yiktib.

5. Negation: ma (separate) + verb-sh(i) (glued)

This is the single highest-frequency structural pattern in the language, so it earns its own section.

Rule (revised): to avoid producing a very large fused cluster, ma is written as its own separate word — the same free-particle treatment as el — while -sh/-esh/-sh(i) stays glued to the verb (and any object clitics on it). This mirrors how Egyptian Arabic is actually written in Arabic script, where ما is its own word and the ـش suffix attaches to the verb. This replaces the earlier fully-fused treatment of the whole circumfix.

Arabic Gloss Tier 1 Tier 2
ما كتبش he didn't write ma katabsh(i) ma katabϣ(i)
ما بيكتبش he doesn't write ma beyektibsh(i) ma beyektibϣ(i)
ما كتبهملیش he didn't write them to me ma katabhomliish ma katabhomliiϣ
مش هيكتب he won't write (future uses a separate word, not the circumfix) mesh hayektib miϣ hayektib
مش كتب؟ didn't he write? (interrogative negation) mesh katab? miϣ katab?

Three fuller examples, showing ma separated before different word classes (a plain verb, a presentative/copula-like word, and a possession verb with object clitic):

  • ماكتبش الواجب ليه؟! — "Why didn't he do the homework?!" Tier 1/2: Ma katabϣ(i) el waageb leeh?!
  • ماهو الحال ده مينفعش — "Well, this situation isn't workable." (ماهو itself can fuse or stay separate — see below; the verbal negation مينفعش stays "ma" + glued verb) Tier 1/2: Mahu (Ma ahu) el ϩaal da ma yenfaⲴsh(i).
  • ما احنا كل شوية معندناش من المنتج ده — "We don't have any of this product every other minute" (i.e. we keep running out) Tier 1/2: Ma eϩna (Maϩna — surface pronunciation in rapid speech) koll ϣwayya ma Ⲵndinaaϣ min el montagg da(h).

Two things worth noting from these: 1. ماهو (ma + huwa) — a presentative/topicalizing "ma," not the verbal negator — has both a fused everyday form (Mahu) and a fuller separated form (Ma ahu); both are correct, the fused one is just the faster, more contracted pronunciation. 2. ما احنا (ma + eḥna) similarly has a careful separated form (Ma eϩna) and a fast-speech fused variant (Maϩna) — this fused variant is a pronunciation note, not a rewrite of the spelling rule: the standard written form keeps ma separate.

5.0a Bare ma (no -sh(i)) — oath/emphatic negation

A separate, valid negation pattern uses ma alone, with no -sh(i) suffix at all — most typically in oaths and emphatic swearing formulas, a more classical/literary-feeling register than the everyday ma-...-sh(i):

  • عليا النعمة ما عملت حاجة! — "By the blessing upon me (I swear), I didn't do a thing!" Tier 1/2: Ⲵalayya el neⲴma ma Ⲵamalt ϩaaga!

Here ma negates Ⲵamalt directly with nothing glued after it — no -sh(i), and no shortening/syncope/epenthesis effects either, since those are specifically triggered by adding -sh(i) (§5.1). This bare-ma pattern is a distinct register choice, not an abbreviation of the ma-...-sh(i) form.

5.1 Phonological knock-on effects (on the glued verb-sh(i) portion)

The source grammar documents several vowel changes triggered by adding -sh to the verb. These stay exactly as documented before — only the spacing of ma relative to the verb has changed, not the internal phonology of the verb+sh(i) unit:

Resolved — spelling_rules id 7 (tier2-rules.json): these three effects (shortening, syncope, epenthesis) are a formal, explicit rule, treated the same way the θ→s and ð→z Cairene mergers are — spelled out, not left implicit:

  • Shortening: اختار extaar → ما اختارش ma khtarsh(i) / ma xtarϣ(i) — the long aa shortens before the single final consonant.
  • Syncope: كبر keber → ما كبرش ma kbersh(i) / ma kberϣ(i) — the short first-syllable vowel drops.
  • Epenthesis: كنت kont → ما كنتش ma kontish(i) / ma kontiϣ(i) — an i is inserted so the word doesn't end in a three-consonant cluster.

Confirmed: the elision still happens even with ma written separately — extaar "he chose" negates to ma xtarsh(i) (not ma extaarsh(i)):

  • الراجل اختار شريكة حياته — "The man chose his spouse" Tier 1/2: El raagel extaar shereeket hayaato.
  • الولد ما اختارش لسه الألوان — "The boy hasn't chosen the colors yet" Tier 1/2: El walad ma xtarsh(i) lessa el alwaan.

6. Word order & syntax notes

These don't change spelling, but they matter for anything downstream that generates or corrects full sentences (the meaning-aware model in particular) — flagging them here so nothing "corrects" a grammatical Masri sentence into MSA word order.

6.1 SVO, not VSO

Masri/Egyptian Arabic prefers Subject–Verb–Object, unlike Classical/MSA's Verb–Subject–Object:

  • عادل قرا الكتاب — "Adel read the book" Tier 1: `aadel ara el kitaab Tier 2: Ⲵaadel ara el kitaab

6.2 Wh-in-situ (question words stay in place — with flexibility)

This is flagged in the source grammar as a likely feature inherited from the Coptic substratum — directly relevant to the project's own legitimacy narrative, since it's an actual documented Coptic-influence claim you can cite rather than assert. That said, wh-in-situ isn't rigid: fronting the question word is also grammatical, giving two valid word orders for the same question.

Arabic Gloss Tier 1 (in-situ) Tier 2 (in-situ) Tier 1/2 (fronted alternative)
راح مصر امتى؟ When did he go to Egypt? raah masr emta? raaϩ maṣr emta? Emta raaϩ maṣr?
راح مصر ليه؟ Why did he go to Egypt? raah masr leih? raaϩ maṣr leeh? Leeh raaϩ maṣr?
مين اللي راح مصر؟ Who went to Egypt? meen elli raah masr? meen elli raaϩ maṣr? — (mein is already clause-initial here)

Compare to MSA, which always fronts. Masri/Egyptian Arabic allows both — worth keeping both orders in mind for anything downstream that generates or corrects full sentences, so a fronted question isn't flagged as wrong.

6.3 Dual agreement collapses to plural

Dual nouns stay grammatically dual in form but trigger plural agreement on verbs, demonstratives, and adjectives:

  • الأستاذين السوريين دول بيمشوا للجامعة — "These two Syrian professors are walking to the university" Tier 1: el ostazéén el soreyyiin dol beyemshu lel gam`a Tier 2: el ostazéén el soreyyiin dool beyemϣu lel gamⲴa

Full dual/plural paradigm for ostaaz (and other professional titles) is in §2.3.

Resolved — spelling_rules id 8: li- and el fuse into their own single word — lel (or lil, an accepted alternate before certain following consonants) — bi- and el fuse into bel, fi- and el fuse into fel, the shortened a/Ⲵa (§3.4) fuses with el into **al/Ⲵal, and the topicalizing/emphatic particle ma (§5, distinct from negation ma) fuses with el into Mal**:

  • ما المهمة دي لازم تخلص يا حضرات — "This task has to get done, ladies and gentlemen" Tier 1/2: Ma el mohemma di laazem texlaṣ ya ϩaḍaraat. (= fused Mal mohemma di laazem texlaṣ ya ϩaḍaraat.)

But that resulting word then behaves exactly like plain el: it stays separate from the noun that follows it, the same free-particle treatment as el itself (§0). So the fusion is preposition/particle+el only — it doesn't extend to the following noun. Not every preposition does this: **and ("to have") + el stays two separate words —and el, never andel** — so this is a closed list of short/light elements (li-, bi-, fi-, the shorteneda-, and this topicalizing ma), not a blanket rule for every preposition or particle.

Important distinction: this Ma+el→Mal fusion applies to the topicalizing/emphatic ma directly preceding el — it does not apply to the negation ma, which stays separate from the verb it negates regardless of what follows (spelling_rules id 10, §5). The two ma's are different words that happen to look identical; whether they fuse with a following el depends on which one it is, not on the letter sequence alone.

  • للجامعة → lel gam`a / lel gamⲴa "to the university"
  • بالكتاب → bel kitaab / bel kitaab "with/by the book"
  • ف الصميم (= في الصميم) "right in the heart of it" → fel ṣamiim (= fuller, unfused fi el ṣamiim)
