Arabic-V — The Vocalized Arabic Representation of Masri

Arabic-V is the canonical, vocalized, Egyptian-oriented Arabic-script representation of Masri — Harakat/Tashkeel, mandatory sukūn, shadda-based doubling, and dedicated Egyptian realization markers, all Unicode-defined. It is not a separate tier or a competing orthography; it is one of the five Masri representations of the single canonical system, alongside Masri Standard, DRS, Masri Basic, and Arabic-P.

Status: Arabic-V is canonical and established. The vowel system was reworked from scratch on IPA quality, and every earlier open vowel item is now decided. Every vowel has one Masri letter and one Arabic-V form: a haraka on the consonant writes a short vowel, and a carrier letter writes a long one. This replaces the earlier reading of وَ and يَ as short vowels, and it supersedes rules AV-V01 to AV-V04 (see §35). This page documents the full rule set — Rule IDs prefixed AV- — plus the Unicode code points and worked examples an implementation needs. Areas still open for formal specification are listed in §36. Every Latin example is written in the Tier 2 alphabet (Masri Standard).

Part I — Foundations

1. Masri and its representations

Masri is one underlying canonical orthographic system with multiple representations.

Representation Purpose
Masri Standard Primary public Latin representation
DRS — Diaa Romanization System Technical Latin representation
Arabic-V Canonical vocalized Arabic-script representation
Masri Basic ASCII-compatible practical fallback
Arabic-P Plain Arabic-script practical fallback

Only Masri Basic / Tier 1 and Masri Standard / Tier 2 are called tiers. The relationship is one canonical Masri system, shown as Masri Standard, DRS, Arabic-V, Masri Basic, and Arabic-P in turn. Arabic-V therefore represents the canonical Masri system in Arabic script; it does not create a different orthography. See the Representations page for how all five relate.

2. What Arabic-V is for

Arabic-V provides an explicit Arabic-script representation for situations where ordinary unvocalized Arabic does not provide enough information. Its purposes include:

  • representing Egyptian pronunciation explicitly
  • showing vowels that Arabic-P leaves implicit
  • showing final consonants with no following vowel
  • representing consonant and vowel doubling
  • preserving underlying letters where useful
  • representing Cairene pronunciation mergers
  • representing Egyptian-specific vowels
  • representing foreign vocabulary according to pronunciation
  • providing an Arabic-script counterpart to Masri Standard
  • supporting linguistic and computational analysis

Arabic-V is therefore not simply Arabic-P + Tashkeel. It is a dedicated Masri representation.

3. Arabic-P versus Arabic-V

Arabic-P — Plain Arabic is ordinary Arabic script without explicit vocalization, e.g. أسانسير. It is familiar, fast, and practical, but pronunciation information may remain implicit.

Arabic-V — Vocalized Arabic explicitly represents the Masri pronunciation, e.g. أَسَنْسْيَرْ. Conceptually, Masri Standard maps to Arabic-V the way it maps to any other representation — rather than asking the reader to infer missing vowels, Arabic-V writes them.

The distinction is Arabic-P → practical and familiar versus Arabic-V → explicit and informative. Arabic-P is not wrong, and Arabic-V is not simply "better Arabic". They serve different purposes.

4. The fundamental Arabic-V principle

Arabic-V follows one central principle:

Write the canonical Masri sequence explicitly.

This means that Arabic-V represents:

  • the consonant
  • its vowel, where applicable
  • doubling, where applicable
  • absence of a following vowel through sukūn
  • Egyptian-specific realization information where required

This makes Arabic-V structurally different from ordinary Arabic spelling. The Tier 2 spelling decides; Arabic-V spells it out.

5. Canonicalization architecture

Arabic-V is generated forward from canonical Masri (Tier 2). The preferred pipeline is:

Egyptian input
  ↓
Canonical Masri
  ↓
Masri Standard / DRS / Arabic-V / Masri Basic / Arabic-P

This prevents Arabic-V from becoming an independent spelling-normalization system. The canonical Masri representation determines consonants, vowels, doubled vowels, consonant gemination, sukūn, final forms, Cairene realization, and foreign-word pronunciation. Arabic-V then expresses those decisions in Arabic script.

Part II — Vowels and vocalization

6. The vowel system

The vowels are defined by IPA quality first, then spelled in Arabic-V.

IPA Sound Masri Example
[u] close back rounded: a pure oo, as in boot o (short), oo (long) borr, boor
[o] close-mid back rounded: a pure oh, the first half of go ou (short), uu (long) louṣt, buur
[ɔ] open-mid back rounded: as in thought and law ou Kours
[ʌ] open-mid back unrounded: as in cup and but u (foreign words) menu
[ɑ] open back unrounded: as in father a, á Baabaa, Fusṭáát
[ɛə] the Egyptian long a aa ketaab

The front vowels keep their letters (e, ee, é, éé); only the Arabic-V forms of é and éé follow the pattern below. ii is always written ee; the one exception is a word-final ii, which stays ii by Latin convention (AV-R09). The long vowels in use are oo, ee, éé, aa, word-final ii and, outside Cairo, uu.

Arabic-V forms are shown as they appear before a following consonant, so sukūn is included where AV-R03 requires it.

7. Carriers and harakat — AV-R01

The vowel carrier letters are ا و ي. Vowel quality is a haraka on the consonant: a َ, e ِ, o ُ, and ou َُ and é َِ with the fatha indicator (AV-R06a). A long vowel adds the carrier with shadda and sukūn (AV-S02): aa َ + اّْ, ee ِ + يّْ, oo ُ + وّْ. The long open vowels have no separate haraka: uu is وَ and éé is يَ, the carrier with the fatha indicator.

Mark Name Function
◌َ Fatḥa a; with damma or kasra it is the fatha indicator (AV-R06a)
◌ِ Kasra e
◌ُ Ḍamma o
◌ْ Sukūn no vowel follows (AV-R03)
◌ّ Shadda doubling (AV-S02, AV-R04)
◌ۜ Small high seen separation mark on و / ي (AV-R05); also the realization marker on ث / ذ (§18–19)

When no consonant precedes the vowel, the initial hamza-alif carries the haraka (AV-R10): إِيّْ, أُوّْ.

The alif madda آ is not canonical. It is accepted as input, read as hamza + aa, and normalized to أَاّْ at the start of a word and to ◌َ + اّْ elsewhere.

8. Word-final vowels — AV-R02 and AV-R02a

AV-R02. Word-final vowels

A word-final short vowel is the bare carrier letter, preceded by a consonant carrying sukūn.

  • final i = يْ: Soori, Damanhoori, Ϣamaasi, Roodi رُوّْدْيْ
  • final long ii = ◌ِ + يّْ (the Latin letters stay ii): katabhomlii كَتَبْهُمْلِيّْ
  • final a = اْ: Huda, sama, the pronoun ending in Ϣamaaseeha
  • final o = وْ, by the same pattern: kebro كِبْرْوْ, kallemo كَلِّمْوْ, Leedo لِيّْدْوْ (Roodi we Leedo رُوّْدْيْ وِ لِيّْدْوْ)
  • final e stays a kasra on the consonant: We وِ

A word-final ي after a consonant with sukūn always means i. It is never e. (A final long ii is a different case: its ي is a carrier, so it carries shadda as well as sukūn.) At the end of a word one Arabic letter then maps to one Latin letter, so a reader never has to choose between values, and final -i is the established Latin-script style for this ending (Soori, Damanhoori, Ϣamaasi). After a vowel-bearing letter or a carrier, the same ي is the consonant y (AV-R07): Ray رَيْ, Kay كَيْ. The position decides, so the rule has no exceptions.

Sukūn on a final ا is written always. It marks a final short, non-geminated a. A long final aa is ◌َ + اّْ.

AV-R02a. ى is input only

The alif maqṣūrah ى is not canonical. Like آ, it is accepted as input and never written. It is pronounced a, so Arabic-V writes the canonical letter ا instead, in the short final position (AV-R02) and in the long form (ىّٰ becomes اّْ).

Arabic Masri (Tier 2) Arabic-V
هدى Huda هُدْاْ
موسى Moosa مُوّْسْاْ

Reasons:

  1. Pronunciation decides. ى in these words is pronounced a, never y or i, so the letter shape is an inherited spelling rather than a sound.
  2. One canonical letter per sound. ا is the canonical letter for a; ى is a non-canonical alternative, the same way tanween is (AV-R04a).
  3. It protects AV-R02. In everyday Egyptian writing a final ى and a final ي are easily confused. Since a final ي always means i, spelling a as ا keeps the two apart.

9. Sukūn — AV-S01 and AV-R03

AV-S01. Mandatory sukūn

When a consonant has no vowel after it, Arabic-V writes sukūn on that consonant. This is a fundamental difference from ordinary Arabic-P. For example, Katab is كَتَبْ rather than simply كتب:

Masri Standard Arabic-V
k كَ
a ◌َ
t تَ
a ◌َ
b بْ

Thus K a t a b → كَتَبْ. The final بْ explicitly tells the reader that no vowel follows /b/. Sukūn is not optional decoration: in fully specified Arabic-V it is required whenever the consonant is not followed by a vowel.

AV-R03. Sukūn extended to carriers

Every letter with no vowel after it takes sukūn. That covers consonants (including one directly before a final short-vowel carrier) and carrier letters:

  • long vowels before a consonant: وّْ يّْ اّْ
  • final short vowels: يْ (i), اْ (a) and وْ (o)

The consonant before a long-vowel carrier carries the haraka of that vowel instead (AV-R01). Letters that carry their own haraka (يَ, وَ, يَۜ, وَۜ) take no sukūn. ة is left unmarked; its construct form تْ is a consonant and takes sukūn.

Examples: Damanhoori دَمَنْهُوّْرْيْ, Kamaan كَمَاّْنْ.

This extends AV-S01 to carriers, so the earlier page examples تَفْكْيّرْ and يْوَدّْي are now written تَفْكِيّْرْ and يُوَۜدّْيْ.

10. Shadda, gemination and tanween — AV-S02, AV-R04, AV-R04a

AV-S02. Shadda as doubling

Shadda represents doubling. The element being doubled depends on what the shadda belongs to. It is best understood as a doubling operator, not merely as a "gemination mark", and it is not a synonym for "long vowel".

  • Consonant doubling. For consonants, Cّ = CC. Example: دّ = dd. In ywaddi → يُوَۜدّْيْ, the دّ is the doubled consonant d + d.
  • Vowel doubling. For vowel carriers, shadda represents a doubled vowel: Vّ = VV. For example, ee → ◌ِ + يّْ (AV-R01).

AV-R04. Gemination is universal

A doubled consonant is always shadda, including word-finally, where shadda is followed by sukūn:

  • Geddann جِدَّنّْ
  • Temmann تِمَّنّْ
  • baladd بَلَدّْ

Mark order is haraka, then shadda, then sukūn, then the separation mark ۜ (AV-R05), whose Unicode combining class (230) puts it last. This is already the canonical Unicode order.

AV-R04a. Tanween becomes ن + shadda

Tanween (ـً ـٌ ـٍ) is not written as a mark in Arabic-V. The /n/ it stands for is written as the actual letter ن, doubled per AV-R04 and followed by sukūn. Credit for this substitution goes to Samy Harak (سامي حرك); see §38.

Arabic with tanween Masri (Tier 2) Arabic-V
جدًّا Geddann جِدَّنّْ
أبدًا Abadann أَبَدَنّْ

Reasons:

  1. Tanween is not canonical. It is a mark, not a letter, and the pronounced /n/ it stands for is not written as a letter anywhere in the source spelling.
  2. It has no single written form. The ending is realized through different seat letters depending on the word (ا ي و ى ء), or it is omitted altogether in unvocalized text.
  3. Masri spells every pronounced sound with a letter. The /n/ is real speech, so it is written as the letter ن, like any other consonant.
  4. The doubling keeps what tanween shows. A final -nn marks the ending as tanween-derived and stays distinct from an ordinary final n (as in Kamaan). It is also just AV-R04 applied: gemination is universal, including word-finally.

11. W and Y: consonant or carrier — AV-R05 and AV-R07

AV-R05. W and Y are consonants; harakat work on two levels

Latin w is always the consonant و; Latin y is always the consonant ي. Examples: We وِ, WaaϨed وَۜاّْحِدْ.

Arabic-script harakat work on two levels, and a small sign says which level applies:

Arabic-V Reads Level
وَۜ wa consonant w + a
يَۜ ya consonant y + a
وَ uu one long vowel
يَ éé one long vowel

The small high seen ۜ (U+06DC) is the separation-of-concerns marker. With it, the letter is a consonant and the fatha is a separate vowel after it (w + a, y + a), including before a long carrier (waa is وَۜاّْ). Without it, the letter and its fatha together are one long vowel (uu, éé). A consonantal و or ي that carries a fatha always takes the mark, so no context is needed to read it.

There is no exception: ywaddi is yuwaddi in a shortened form, يُوَۜدّْيْ, the يُ (u) followed by wa.

AV-R07. Carrier versus consonant

A carrier is immediately preceded by a consonant with sukūn, or by an initial hamza-alif. A و or ي is a consonant when it follows a vowel-bearing consonant, follows a carrier, begins a word, or carries a haraka other than the fatha indicator.

Example: the ي in Hedaayet follows a carrier, so it is consonantal.

A consonant with sukūn followed by وَ or يَ is a long vowel: t + uu (تْوَ), b + éé (بْيَ). Followed by وَۜ or يَۜ it is a consonant + a: t + wa (تْوَۜ), b + ya (بْيَۜ). The separation mark settles every case (AV-R05).

12. The vowels o, ou, uu, u, é and éé — AV-R06 and AV-R06a

AV-R06

o and oo are the close [u]: damma, and damma plus the doubled carrier. borr بُرّْ, boor بُوّْرْ. The rounded vowel of look and boot is always plain o.

ou and uu are the close-mid [o], with the open-mid [ɔ] as a variant of ou (course, law). Short ou is the fatha indicator on the damma: louṣt لَُصْتْ, Kours كَُرْسْ. Long uu is the carrier with the fatha indicator: buur بْوَرْ, Farduus فَرْدْوَسْ. The u shows that the o is the [o] of toe and not the [u] of look. Long [o] is not normative Cairene. It is normal in the Canal dialects (Port Said) and in Damietta, where it is written uu, not ouou.

é and éé. Short é is the fatha indicator on the kasra; long éé is the carrier with the fatha indicator: asanséér أَسَنْسْيَرْ. Short é is in kaféhaat كَفَِهَاّْتْ. The Egyptian /ɛ/ is thus no longer written by a bare يَ; that form is the long éé.

u (IPA [ʌ]) is not pronounced naturally in Egyptian speech and is reserved for foreign words, where it is the fast you (yw): يُ. menu (menyw, منيو in plain Arabic) is مِنْيُ. The same يُ is why ywaddi is yuwaddi in a shortened form (AV-R05).

AV-R06a. The fatha indicator

A fatha written together with a damma or a kasra on the same consonant is the fatha indicator. It turns damma into ou and kasra into é, and it is what separates these short vowels from the long uu and éé that the carrier writes: لَُ (lou) against لْوَ (luu). Mark order is fatha, then damma or kasra, which is already the canonical Unicode order.

13. The feminine ending — AV-R08

The taa marbūṭa ة is written when the word is isolated or final: the ending is -a. When something follows and is added to the word (the next word of a construct, or a suffix), the ending becomes -et and Arabic-V writes it as the letter ت with sukūn, as ordinary Arabic does. The e is a kasra on the letter before it, because ت is a plain consonant, whereas ة carries its own vowel.

-eyya and -eyyet are written as a kasra on the preceding consonant, then يّ, then ة (free) or يِّ and تْ (with an addition).

Masri (Tier 2) Arabic-V
Toumeyya تَُمِيّة
Tekeyya تِكِيّة
Tekeyyet el baladd تِكِيِّتْ اِلْبَلَدّْ
Hedaaya هِدَاّْية
Hedaayet el sama هِدَاّْيِتْ اِلْسَمْاْ

The consonantal ي before ة carries no mark of its own, exactly like the يّ of -eyya, because the ة carries the a: Hedaaya هِدَاّْية.

14. No medial i; ii is written ee — AV-R09

In Egyptianized words, Latin i occurs only at the start or end of a word. Every medial i is replaced by e. A medial short front vowel is e and a medial long one is ee: Ϣamaaseeha شَمَاّْسِيّْهْاْ. ii is always written ee. The one exception is a word-final ii, which stays ii by Latin convention; its Arabic-V form is the same as ee (◌ِ + يّْ). Example: katabhomlii كَتَبْهُمْلِيّْ, but katabhomleeϣ كَتَبْهُمْلِيّْشْ once a suffix follows.

Loanword citation forms keep their usual spelling and may keep a medial i (escript, printer, parking); once a loanword is Egyptianized it follows the rule above (piano → biano, plastic → blastik, pizza → bitza). An initial E is the first step of Egyptianizing a loanword.

15. Initial vowels — AV-R10 and AV-R11

AV-R10. Initial vowel letter

A word-initial vowel is written with a hamza form (إِ, أَ, أُ) only when the underlying word has a real hamza. The article el and a prothetic vowel take a bare ا with a haraka: اِلْ, اِسْكْرِپْتْ.

Initial hamza is silent and unwritten in Tier 2, so this distinction comes from the underlying spelling, not from the Latin.

AV-R11. Initial I versus E

Both are written إِ. The Tier 2 letter is decided by scanning the next two consonant letters after the initial vowel. Vowel letters are skipped. If either consonant is a lowering letter the vowel is E; otherwise it is I.

Part III — Consonants

16. Consonant letters — AV-R12

Consonants follow the Standard Masri Alphabet: J is always چ (Masri's standard, like P پ and V ڤ), and G is ج (Cairene hard /g/).

17. Cairene Qāf → Hamza — AV-C01

Arabic-V distinguishes between ق = /q/ and قٔ = Cairene /ʔ/. The marked form retains the underlying ق while explicitly indicating its Cairene realization as a glottal stop. The marker is U+0654 — ARABIC HAMZA ABOVE.

Therefore ق + U+0654 means: underlying ق, realized as /ʔ/ in Cairene. This is information-preserving and is different from simply replacing ق with ء.

18. Cairene ث → /s/ — AV-C02

Arabic-V may preserve underlying ث while explicitly marking its Cairene realization as /s/. The marker is U+06DC — ARABIC SMALL HIGH SEEN. Thus ث + U+06DC means: underlying ث, realized as Egyptian /s/. The base letter remains visible.

19. Cairene ذ → /z/ — AV-C03

Likewise, ذ + U+06DC means: underlying ذ, realized as Egyptian /z/.

Base Marker Meaning
ث U+06DC underlying ث → /s/
ذ U+06DC underlying ذ → /z/

The marker itself does not represent a phoneme independently. Its meaning comes from the combination base letter + realization marker.

20. Underlying-preserving versus fully merged spelling

Arabic-V allows an important distinction. An Egyptian /z/ that historically corresponds to ذ may be represented either as ذ + merger marker, or, where permitted by the canonical rules, as ز. The first preserves the underlying identity; the second is the simpler fully merged representation.

Therefore the system can provide both an information-preserving representation and a simplified Egyptian representation, without treating either approach as inherently "wrong". The final normative rules should specify which form is preferred in each context.

21. Masri J — AV-C04

Arabic-V uses چ for the Masri letter J. Its sound value is /ʒ/. Therefore J → چ → /ʒ/. Example: Rooj → رُوّْچْ (plain Arabic-P: روچ). This provides an Arabic-script representation that maintains the distinction represented by Masri J.

22. Foreign C/c — AV-F01

The Latin letter C/c does not have one fixed Masri value in foreign words. Its Arabic-V representation follows its actual pronunciation.

Pronunciation Example Arabic-P form (consonant choice shown) Result
C = /k/ Caterpillar كاترپيلر C → ك
C = /s/ center سنتر C → س

Therefore C + /k/ → ك and C + /s/ → س. C must never be converted solely from its spelling.

23. Foreign X/x — AV-F02

Arabic-V distinguishes canonical Masri X/x from foreign Latin x/X. The canonical Masri letter is X → خ → /x/. But a foreign x/X is interpreted according to its pronunciation.

Pronunciation Example Arabic-P form (consonant choice shown) Result
foreign x = /ks/ Taxi تاكسي x → ك + س
foreign X = /z/ Xenophon زينوفون X → ز
Context Arabic-V
Masri X/x خ
foreign x = /ks/ كس
foreign X = /z/ ز

The converter must never use a universal X → خ substitution.

Part IV — Reference

24. Practical example

Masri Standard: Sayyed katab el dars.

Arabic-V: سَيِّدْ كَتَبْ اِلْدَرْسْ

The representation is pronunciation-oriented and is intended to reflect Egyptian pronunciation, not MSA pronunciation.

Word 1 — Sayyed. سَيِّدْ is س + ◌َ + ي + ◌ّ + ◌ِ + د + ◌ْ. The consonantal ي follows a vowel-bearing consonant (AV-R07), carries the shadda for yy (AV-R04) and the kasra for e; the final دْ has sukūn.

Word 2 — Katab. كَتَبْ is the sequence ك + ◌َ + ت + ◌َ + ب + ◌ْ, i.e. K a t a b. The final بْ has mandatory sukūn because there is no vowel after /b/.

Word 3 — el dars. اِلْدَرْسْ — the article takes a bare ا with kasra (AV-R10), then لْ with sukūn, then دَرْسْ with sukūn on both final consonants.

The complete sentence is therefore:

سَيِّدْ كَتَبْ اِلْدَرْسْ

This is a practical demonstration of what makes Arabic-V different from ordinary Arabic-P.

30. Information-preservation principle

Arabic-V should not discard information unnecessarily. For example, قٔ contains more information than ء, because it communicates both the underlying ق and its Cairene /ʔ/ realization. Likewise ثۜ contains more information than س when the underlying ث matters, and ذۜ contains more information than ز when the underlying ذ matters.

This makes Arabic-V useful not only for reading, but also for normalization, linguistic analysis, machine learning, conversion between representations, and pronunciation-aware processing.

Scope. The principle governs Cairene realization markers, where an underlying letter differs from its pronounced value. It does not apply to inherited spelling variants that carry no difference in sound: آ, ى and tanween marks are accepted as input and normalized (AV-R01, AV-R02a, AV-R04a).

32. Arabic-V versus phonetic transcription

Arabic-V is not unrestricted IPA written in Arabic letters. It remains an orthographic representation of canonical Masri.

For example, قٔ does not merely mean "write the sound /ʔ/". It means: the canonical underlying letter is ق, and its Cairene realization is /ʔ/. Similarly, ثۜ means: the canonical underlying letter is ث, realized as /s/. This is why Arabic-V is an orthography rather than a simple pronunciation transcription.

36. Areas requiring formal specification

The Arabic-V framework is established. The vowel system, sukūn, shadda and gemination, tanween, final-vowel behavior, hamza/alif behavior, definite article behavior and grammatical endings are now decided (§6–§15). The complete technical standard should continue formalizing:

  • loanword rules beyond the vowel and consonant rules above
  • proper names beyond the examples in §28
  • additional Egyptian mergers
  • additional foreign sounds
  • Unicode normalization
  • renderer requirements

No rule should be inferred merely from conventional MSA spelling.

37. Decision log and companion decisions

Vowel-rework decisions (all closed).

  1. Consonantal w and y followed by a. وَۜ (wa) and يَۜ (ya) take the small high seen (AV-R05). ywaddi is yuwaddi in a shortened form and needs no exception.
  2. uu, ii and u. ii is always written ee; the one exception is a word-final ii, which stays ii by Latin convention (AV-R09). uu is the long [o], written وَ, and is normal outside Cairo. u is the fast you of foreign words, يُ (AV-R06).
  3. Final and suffix details. A final short o is وْ (AV-R02). -aaya is اّْية, with the ي unmarked before ة, and -aayet is اّْيِتْ (AV-R08).
  4. Word-initial long aa. آ is input only. The canonical form is the hamza-alif with fatha plus the doubled carrier, أَاّْ (AV-R01).
  5. Page examples updated. The examples on this page now read تَفْكِيّْرْ and يُوَۜدّْيْ.
  6. Word-final consonantal y. After a vowel-bearing letter or a carrier a final ي is the consonant y; after a consonant with sukūn it is i (AV-R02, AV-R07).
  7. Short ou and é. Short ou and é are the fatha indicator on the damma and kasra; long uu and éé are the carrier with the fatha indicator (AV-R06, AV-R06a).
  8. Medial i. Every medial i in an Egyptianized word is written e. Loanword citation forms keep their usual spelling (escript, printer, parking, vidyu, vitrina), and Egyptianized loanwords use their usual forms (bitza, biano, blastik) (AV-R09). The Tier 1, Tier 2 and DRS spellings of native words on the site were updated to match.

Companion decisions made in the same session.

  • Tier 1 (applied on the Alphabet page). ح is h^ in Tier 1 and Ϩ in Tier 2 (AϨmad). The glottal stop is ' in Tier 1, written only mid-word and word-finally, never at the start (Ma'moor, Islaam). If a digraph-looking pair must be pronounced as two letters, a hyphen goes between them in Tier 1 (Mashhoor مشهور, Mas-h^oor مسحور).
  • Arabic script (applied on the site). J is always چ; P is always پ; V is always ڤ; G stays ج.

40. Final definition

Arabic-V is Masri's canonical vocalized Arabic-script representation: a dedicated Egyptian-oriented orthography that combines Arabic letters, Harakat, mandatory sukūn where required, shadda-based doubling, and defined Masri-specific letters, vowel conventions, and realization markers. Tanween is not written as a mark; the /n/ it stands for is written as the letter ن.

Its fundamental principle is:

One canonical Masri system, multiple controlled representations.

Arabic-V therefore provides an Arabic-script representation that is recognizable, Egyptian-oriented, explicitly vocalized, information-preserving, pronunciation-aware, computationally useful, and extensible.

Source: this page documents the project's Arabic-V specification in full. The condensed marks and markers are also indexed as data in assets/data/arabic-v.json for lookup/tooling use; see the Representations page for how Arabic-V relates to Masri Standard, DRS, Masri Basic, and Arabic-P.
